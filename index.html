Dead Pattern,
Live Tension

A cooperative card game about order of operations. The patterns are not the hard part. Knowing which one this situation can bear, right now, is the hard part — and the game is built so that a correct pattern played at the wrong moment does visible damage three rounds later.

4–5 players (scales 3–8) 45–70 min per situation 29 cards · 3 situations One player keeps the situation

Print this page (Cmd/Ctrl-P, letter, margins 10mm). Card sheets start on their own pages, 3 across. Cut on the dashed lines. The Keeper panels print on the situation pages — fold or cut those off before the table sees them.

01

What the game is testing

Every card here is a real Sociocracy 3.0 pattern, worded to be usable in an actual room. The game adds one thing S3 does not hand you: a situation with a tolerance, and consequences that arrive late.

Three claims are on trial. If they hold at your table, the design works and the rest is production. If they don't, the numbers are wrong, not the thesis — tell me which one broke.

  1. Novices over-build. Given a hot tension and high trust, new players reach for structure — a Circle, a role, a decision — and ossify a situation that needed almost nothing structural at all.
  2. The same sequence inverts. A line of play that wins one situation loses another for a reason that is not bad luck. There is no strongest opening.
  3. Diagnosis is a real cost. Spending capacity to look instead of act feels like waste in the moment and pays for itself. This is the move facilitation never rewards, and the one the game exists to teach.

One design rule worth knowing before you read the cards: every card states exactly what it does. No card states when it is wrong. Effects are printed because you should be able to reason. Thresholds are hidden because you should have to read the room.

02

The four dials

The situation is four numbers, each 0–5, plus a capacity budget. Everything you play moves them.

HEAT
How live the tension still is. Hot tension is workable; you need it. Heat reaching 0 ends the game — the situation has calcified and no pattern can reach it. Making decisions cools it.
LEGIBILITY
How much of what is happening is actually visible to the people in it.
TRUST
Relational credit. Low trust doesn't block structure; it makes structure land as imposition.
RIGIDITY
Accumulated structure. It only goes up — one card in the deck reduces it. Every situation has a hidden Rigidity Cap. Exceed it and the game ends immediately: ossified.
CAPACITY
What the group can actually spend this round. Refreshes each round to the situation's base, plus or minus any ongoing modifiers. Unspent capacity does not carry over.

Rigidity is the whole engine. It is not a penalty track — it is what structure is. Every structural pattern in S3 is genuinely good and genuinely adds rigidity, and the cap is how much this particular group can carry before its own scaffolding stops it from responding to anything new.

03

A round

  1. Drift. The Keeper turns the next Drift card and applies it. Drift is structural, not villainous — attention decays, people leave, load arrives, process reasserts. It happens whether or not you deserve it.
  2. Declare. The group names up to two of the seven principles this round's plays will advance. Write them down before playing anything. This is the scoring move and it is where the learning is.
  3. Draw. Each player draws to a hand of five from the pattern deck. Draw is generous on purpose: you are meant to read many more cards than you play.
  4. Play. In any order, spending from one shared capacity pool. Discuss freely. A card needs its prerequisites in play and, for some cards, the right holder.
  5. Resolve. The Keeper applies effects, then reveals which principles actually advanced. Score it. Discard down to three; unplayed cards go to the bottom.

You may revise the declaration mid-round once, for 1 capacity. That is the advanced move: noticing your read was wrong while you can still act on it.

04

Declaring, and the seven principles

You do not score for fixing the situation. You score for reading it correctly.

  • Each declared principle that actually advanced this round: +2 Insight.
  • Each declared principle that did not: 0, and tick it on the sheet. The principles you keep declaring and missing are your blind spots in the topology — that column is the most useful thing on the score sheet.
  • Each Retrospective played: +1 Insight.

The group either brings the situation to viability or it doesn't — that part is pass/fail and shared. Insight is how well you saw what you were doing while you did it. A table can lose the situation with a high Insight score, and that is a better evening than winning on luck.

The seven principles are the scoring axes, not cards. They are the topology of the paradigm: swap them out and you have a different deck. Effectiveness · Consent · Empiricism · Continuous Improvement · Equivalence · Transparency · Accountability. The Keeper's table on the situation sheet says which plays advance which.

05

Standings, and the cost of the right person

Deal one Standing card to each player at setup. Some patterns name a holder. A pattern played by the wrong person still resolves — it just resolves worse, because that is what happens.

Route to the Holder costs 1 capacity. Getting the right person into the room takes time, and sometimes you correctly decide you cannot afford it. That trade is the point of the mechanic.

Scaling. 4 players: drop External Stakeholder. 3 players: use Initiator, Steward, Newcomer. 6–8: split into two sub-groups working the same situation from one capacity pool, each with its own Steward — which is also the honest way to discover what Double Linking is for.

06

Ending

The game ends early, and badly, if either of these happens:

  • Calcified — Heat reaches 0. You cooled the tension before you understood it.
  • Ossified — Rigidity exceeds the situation's cap. You built more structure than this group could carry.

Otherwise, at the end of the last round the Keeper reads the viability conditions. Meet all of them and the situation is viable: the group wins together. Fall short on any and it isn't — the Keeper says which, and that sentence is usually the best thing said all evening.

The Lattice

The Lattice6 people · repair-and-reuse node · Ward 4, coastal district

Three years on friendship and a shared bench. Lately two of the six have been making calls the others hear about afterward, from someone else. Nobody is angry. Everyone is a little tired of not knowing, and nobody wants to be the one who turns this into a meeting.

HEAT 4LEG 1TRUST 4RIG 0

Capacity 5 / round5 roundsIn play: nothing

Keeper panel — fold before play

Rigidity Cap 3. Viability: LEG ≥ 4 · TRUST ≥ 4 · HEAT ≥ 2.

Drift order: Attention Decay → Quiet Capture → Attention Decay → Cooling → Someone Writes It Down

This situation forgives one structural step and kills the second. A domain plus a consent decision lands at Rigidity 3 with the Quiet Capture drift — exactly at cap, and viable. A Circle on top is 5 and the game ends. There are two winning lines here, which is deliberate: the lesson is not "never build", it is "this group can carry one thing".

Narrate the ossification when it comes, don't just announce it: the bench now has a rota nobody consented to, two people have stopped coming on Thursdays, and the thing they were actually worried about has never once been said out loud.

The Ward

The Ward~40 people · three crews · municipal water commons

A procurement scandal took out the coordinating body eight months ago. The three crews still run the filtration beds and no longer share numbers with each other. Everyone is professional. Nothing is hidden exactly, and nothing is visible either. The tension is not escalating — it is cooling into something that will soon be called "how it is".

Capacity 5 / round6 roundsIn play: nothing

Keeper panel — fold before play

Rigidity Cap 6. Viability: LEG ≥ 4 · TRUST ≥ 3 · HEAT ≥ 2 · AGREEMENT in play.

Drift order: Cooling → Attention Decay → Extraction Pressure → Turnover → Cooling → Quiet Capture

Here they must build — restraint alone cannot reach an agreement, and two Cooling drifts punish waiting. But Trust 1 means structure played early lands as imposition, and every Consent decision costs Heat. The winning shape is trust and legibility first, then exactly one real agreement, then evaluate it.

If they open with Consent Decision Making, do not warn them. The purest demonstration in the kit is a table that decides its way to Heat 0 by round three, having done nothing but correct S3 the entire time.

The Interchange

The Interchange~120 people · inherited freight-and-repair federation

It works. Every handoff is logged, every form filed, every circle meets on schedule. The agreements that run it were consented to by people who have all since left, and nobody currently here can say what the federation is for. When you ask, people describe the process. The tension is not hot. It is underneath the floor.

The first drift puts them at Rigidity 6 — on the cap, before anyone plays anything. Capacity is generous and almost nothing is safe to spend it on. The sequence that wins The Ward ossifies this in round three.

This is the situation to run second, not last. Its lesson lands hardest immediately after a table has been rewarded for building well.

09

Running it

You are not a GM and not an adversary. You hold the hidden numbers, apply drift, answer Read the Room honestly, and narrate what the situation does in response. Rotate the job between situations so everyone gets to play.

What to watch for

  • Does anyone spend on Read the Room in round one? This is the rarest move and the most valuable. If no table ever pays for it, it is priced wrong — tell me.
  • Does the table reach for Circle in The Lattice? That is claim one, live.
  • How long before someone notices Evaluate & Evolve is the only card that reduces Rigidity? Time it. That moment is the game's thesis arriving on its own.
  • Does anyone route to the holder, or do they all just play it wrong and eat it? If routing is never worth 1 capacity, the price is wrong.

Debrief — the part that transfers

  1. Which card did you most want to play early and hold back? What told you to hold?
  2. Name the moment the situation stopped telling you things. What had you just played?
  3. Whose Standing did you route around, and what did it cost you?
  4. Which principle did you keep declaring and keep missing?
  5. Where in your actual work is the Rigidity Cap lower than you have been assuming?

Question five is the whole reason to spend an evening on this. Leave time for it.
